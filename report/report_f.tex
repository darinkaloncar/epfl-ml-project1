\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We predict coronary heart disease (MICHD) from the 2015 Behavioral Risk Factor
Surveillance System (BRFSS), a telephone survey of over 300{,}000 US adults with
321 coded answers each. Only 8.8\% of respondents are positive, so we optimize
F1 and tune the decision threshold. We first build regularized logistic
regression trained with Newton's method as a baseline (test F1 0.439). We then
decode the survey using its codebook, recovering units, skip-logic answers and
missingness patterns, and train our own gradient-boosted decision trees in
NumPy, which reach a cross-validated F1 of $0.4435 \pm 0.0038$ and a test F1 of
0.451.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Coronary heart disease is a leading cause of death~\cite{cdcHeartDisease}, and many of its risk
factors (age, hypertension, smoking, diabetes, inactivity) are captured by
self-reported lifestyle surveys. Our task is to predict whether a BRFSS
respondent has ever been diagnosed with MICHD, i.e.\ heart attack or angina.

The data is hard for three reasons. (i) It is a raw survey, not a clean
feature matrix: answers mix units (e.g.\ weight in pounds or kilograms),
packed encodings (``1xx'' per week, ``2xx'' per month), and special codes for
``don't know'' or ``refused''. (ii) Many questions are only asked after a
filter question, so most missing values carry information instead of being
random. (iii) The classes are imbalanced (8.8\% positive), which makes
accuracy misleading and F1 sensitive to the decision threshold.

We describe our feature processing in Sec.~\ref{sec:data}, a regularized
logistic regression baseline in Sec.~\ref{sec:linear}, and our gradient-boosted
trees in Sec.~\ref{sec:gbdt}, and compare them in Sec.~\ref{sec:compare}.

\section{Feature Processing}
\label{sec:data}

The final GBDT pipeline uses codebook-based preprocessing~\cite{cdcBRFSS2015Codebook};
the logistic baseline uses the separate processing described
in Section~\ref{sec:linear}. Identifiers are excluded from
predictors and used to align observations with labels.

We map feature-specific unknown and refused responses to
NaN, encode binary answers as 1/0, and convert packed
frequencies to common units. Missing follow-ups are filled
when a preceding response determines their value: for example,
no alcohol consumption implies zero drinking quantities.
Other missing values remain NaN.

We merge household adult counts across telephone
questionnaires and derive diabetes duration, an indicator
of HIV testing within five years, and a cognitive-impact
score averaging the available responses across three reversed
response scales.
\texttt{UNSURE\_CHRONIC} indicates an explicit ``don't know''
response to any of eleven chronic-condition questions.

We remove administrative variables, survey weights, selected
optional-module questions, and redundant representations,
retaining 176 features. The final tree pipeline applies no
additional percentile capping or imputation. Histogram
boundaries are fitted on training observations and reused
for test data, with a separate bin for missing values.
Nominal variables are marked for categorical splitting.
\texttt{HAREHAB1} is excluded from predictors and used only
in the override described in Section~\ref{sec:compare}.



\section{Baseline: Regularized Logistic Regression}
\label{sec:linear}

Our first model minimizes the logistic loss with an $L_2$ penalty,
$\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \ell(y_i, \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{w}) + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$.
Gradient descent needed hundreds of steps to converge, so we use Newton's method:
each step solves $(\frac{1}{N}X^\top S X + 2\lambda I)\,\Delta\mathbf{w} = \nabla L$,
with $S = \mathrm{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))$, and the loss stops changing after about six
steps. A linear model cannot handle missing values or unordered categories by
itself, so we impute missing values with the training median, add a binary
``was missing'' indicator per feature, one-hot encode categorical columns, add
squared terms for continuous columns, and standardize all features. We choose
$\lambda \in \{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}\}$ and the decision threshold jointly
by 5-fold cross-validation on F1. This model reaches a test F1 of 0.439
(Table~\ref{tab:compare}).

\section{Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees}
\label{sec:gbdt}

The idea of GBDT~\cite{friedman01gbm} is to build an additive model on the log odds, $s(\mathbf{x}) = s_0 + \eta\sum_{k=1}^K{f_k(\mathbf{x})}$,
where $f_k(\mathbf{x})$ represents $k$-th tree, and $s_0$ represents base rate of the positive class, that is:
\begin{equation*}
  s_0 = \log{\frac{\hat{p}}{1-\hat{p}}}, \quad \hat{p} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}{\mathbf{1}\{y_i = 1\}}.
\end{equation*}

The goal of the method is to have a sequence of trees that would sequentially improve the scores. At each round, we compute for every single sample the gradient $g = p - y$, where $p = \sigma(s(\mathbf{x}))$ and the Hessian $h = p(1 - p)$, and fit one regression tree to all of them. The intuition is as follows: Suppose that we have $y = 1$ and current prediction probability $p = 0.3$, it's clear to see that the gradient will be given by $g = -0.7$ which means that we should increase the score. Similarly, the Hessian represents the curvature, or the confidence in our prediction, as we can see that the expression will be maximized as $p \rightarrow \frac{1}{2}$, and is minimized for confident predictions $p \rightarrow \{0,1\}$. 

At each leaf we take a Newton-step $-\frac{G_\ell}{H_\ell + \lambda_{\text{leaf}}}$, where $G_\ell$ and $H_\ell$ are the sums of $g$ and $h$ over that leaf. This is the result of minimizing the second-order Taylor expansion over parameter $w$ of the expression:
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_i \ell(y_i, s_i + w) \approx \text{const} + G\,w + \tfrac12 (H + \lambda)\, w^2,
\end{equation*}
where $G = \sum g_i,\; H = \sum h_i$~\cite{chen16xgboost}.

Each tree outputs $w_\text{leaf}$ for all the samples in it. We can see that splitting a node into left and right children gives us the following advantage:
\begin{equation*}
  \text{gain} = \tfrac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}\right],
\end{equation*}
where $L$ and $R$ indicate left and right child, respectively. We only split a node if both children have $H_L, H_R \geq H_{min}$, so that we do not create trees for small number of already confident samples.

Finally, since finding the splitting thresholds would be too slow if we took into account all the possible thresholds, we decide on binning our values inside $64$ bins, where bin $0$ corresponds to NaN values, while the other $63$ bins are computed on the train set and are ordered. For ordered values, the bins smaller than the threshold go to the left child and the larger ones to the right, while the $0$-th bin can go either way. For categorical bins, we use the trick from \cite{ke17lightgbm} so that we overcome combinatorial nature of all possible permutations. 

We fit each tree on $70\%$ random rows and columns (features), with learning rate $\eta = 0.05$, $H_{min} = 10$, and regularization parameter $\lambda_{\text{leaf}} = 1$. All randomness is seeded, so the submission is exactly reproducible.

\subsection{Results}

To test the GBDT model we use 5-fold CV. To determine the number of trees we use early stopping with patience 50 epochs, on 10\% hold-out of the current training fold, which also serves to choose the F1-maximizing decision threshold. Since this hold-out contains only $\approx$2{,}300 positives, the threshold is noisy (0.20--0.25 across folds); for the final model we therefore also choose it on the pooled out-of-fold predictions of the other four folds, which never uses the scored fold. Table~\ref{tab:gbdt} summarizes the results.

For features, we started with 97 processed features which were first used in the linear model, where GBDT achieves F1 of $0.4403$, already greater than all logistic variants we previously tried. Additionally, giving it all 176 recoded columns from preprocessing with NaNs kept (NaNs are in the previously mentioned $0$-th bin.) increases F1 by $+0.002$ and lowers average log-loss $0.2190 \rightarrow 0.2181$. With the threshold from the other folds, the best value is $\tau = 0.21$ in every fold and we achieve F1 of $0.4435 \pm 0.0038$ across 5 folds using $\tau$.

Regarding hyperparameters, we do small search on fold 0. We find that depth 3 is too shallow for different interactions, and depths 4/5 are very close to each other (these results hold for learning rate $\eta = 0.1$). Lowering $\eta = 0.05$, changes the picture such that now depth 4 achieves the lowest log-loss, and for this depth the folds stop at $464$ trees on average.

For this model (depth 4, $\eta = 0.1$) adding raw leftover columns decreases log-loss by no more than $10^{-4}$, with F1 difference around $\pm 0.002$, which are not always followed by the sign of log-loss difference.

The final model trained on the full training set is constructed with $500$ trees (to account for an increase in data) and achieves \textbf{F1 0.451} and \textbf{Accuracy 0.877}, for threshold $\tau = 0.21$.

\section{Comparison}
\label{sec:compare}

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Test-set (AIcrowd) results. The all-negative accuracy is
  estimated from the training positive rate.}
  \label{tab:compare}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
    \toprule
    Model & F1 & Accuracy \\
    \midrule
    Always predict $-1$ & 0 & $\approx$0.91 \\
    Reg.\ logistic regression (Newton) & 0.439 & 0.876 \\
    GBDT + \texttt{HAREHAB1} override & \textbf{0.451} & \textbf{0.877} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

On the hidden test set, GBDT improves F1 over our best regularized logistic
regression from 0.439 to 0.451 (Table~\ref{tab:compare}). Accuracy stays
essentially unchanged (0.876 vs.\ 0.877) and is below that of the trivial
all-negative classifier, which shows why accuracy is uninformative here.
In the GBDT model, we force the prediction to positive for every respondent
who answered \texttt{HAREHAB1} (cardiac rehabilitation): this question is only asked of
heart-attack survivors, and all 773 such training respondents are positive.
We do not apply this rule to the logistic model, which labels only 154 of the
238 such test respondents as positive. Applying the same rule would raise its
F1 to roughly 0.444, still below the GBDT model, so the rule alone does not explain the
gap between the two models.
